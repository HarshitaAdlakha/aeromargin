Why now

  • Adoption is ahead of policy. Engineers already paste problems into chat models, mostly without company approval (Molex, above). Leadership cannot see where the models are wrong.
  • Regulators are writing the rules. The FAA published its first Roadmap for AI Safety Assurance in 2024. EASA's AI Roadmap 2.0 and its follow-up concept paper set out assurance expectations. Tool-qualification standards such as DO-330 were written for deterministic tools.
  • Frontier labs pay for expert evals. The market for expert-built data and evals (micro1, Mercor, Surge, Scale, Snorkel) is now measured in billions. Narrow, high-consequence domains such as certification-grade engineering are where generic evals say the least.

Where AeroMargin fits

Existing aerospace benchmarks test knowledge recall (multiple choice, true/false) or language tasks. AeroMargin tests the expert step: picking the governing method and its sign before calculating, graded deterministically, with a reference solution and known traps. It is small enough to run in minutes for a few dollars, so a team can rerun it before trusting a new model in its workflow. The same format can be extended to any engineering group's own hand-calculation methods.

Business uses

  • Model selection for engineering teams: which model, at which reasoning setting, is safe to use as a checker's assistant, and on which task types.
  • Training data for labs: each failure mode here (sign reversals, regime switches, reporting conventions) can be extended into hundreds of graded variants by changing the numbers, in the style of GSM-Symbolic.
  • Assurance evidence: a reproducible, deterministic test record fits the "learned AI" assurance thinking in the FAA and EASA roadmaps.
